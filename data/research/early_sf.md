# San Francisco before the Gold Rush, 1769–1849

Research notes for `data/tables/early_sf.csv`. They cover the Yelamu villages, the Spanish mission and presidio, the Mexican ranchos, the pueblo of Dolores, and Yerba Buena up to the first gold-rush year.

The short answer to the owner's question is **no**. Mission Dolores was not empty before 1841. Between 1776 and 1834 it was the largest settlement on the peninsula. At its peak it held 1,000 to 1,250 people, with a church, a padres' house, a court of workshops and eight or more rows of adobe houses for its Indian population. After secularization in 1834 it became a Californio village ("pueblo de Dolores"), and by the 1850s it had more than 50 adobes. Yerba Buena was laid out from 1835 and had about 50 people and half a dozen to a dozen buildings until 1845. It reached about 50 buildings in mid-1846, 157 in August 1847 and about 200 in April 1848, before the gold rush emptied it.

Two items are excluded from the CSV because the landmark modeller covers them: the **Presidio quadrangle and Castillo de San Joaquín**, and the **Mission Dolores church**. Their dimensions are given in section 4.

## 2. Narrative

### Before 1776: the Yelamu
The northern peninsula belonged to the **Yelamu**, a local tribe of the Ramaytush (San Francisco Bay Costanoan) Ohlone. Milliken (2009) puts them at "no more than 160" people, or about 200. His 1995 range was 160–300. They moved seasonally within three or four village clusters (Milliken 2009 pp.3–4 and App. B; Kamiya):

| Group | Winter village | Summer village | Location used in the CSV |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mission Creek (headman **Guimas**) | **Sitlintac**, on the bay shore at the mouth of Mission Creek, "just south of AT&T Park" | **Chutchui**, on Mission Creek "within a few hundred yards of the mission", 2–3 miles inland | Steamboat Point (4th & Berry); Dolores Creek between Guerrero and Valencia, 17th–18th |
| Visitacion (the largest group) | **Tubsinte (Tubsinthe)**, on the cove south of Candlestick; probably the Crocker Mound, CA-SFR-7 | **Amuctac**, in Visitacion Valley | ~1/3 mile SW of Candlestick Park, off Harney Way; lower Visitacion Creek |
| Golden Gate | **Petlenuc**, near the beach facing the Golden Gate | — | Crissy Field. NPS has two camps there: east of the marsh and under Sports Basement. |

Milliken also names a fourth cluster, "Yelamu", which cannot be located. **Shiti**, an abandoned village on Islais Creek recorded by the Spanish (BVHP p.22), is also unlocated. Ompuromo lay SW of Lake Merced, probably in San Mateo County.

**Houses** were domed or conical, built on bent-willow frames thatched with tule bundles, and about 2–6 m across. They were burned and rebuilt when they got dirty. Villages also had **temescales**: semi-subterranean, earth-covered sweat houses beside water. Of 1776 San Francisco, Font recorded only "a little hut with four small children" on the Presidio beach (28 Mar 1776), which the CSV assigns to Petlenuc. The CSV gives each village 5–10 houses, about 41 in all. That figure is an estimate: roughly 160–200 people at 6–8 per house, spread over paired seasonal villages.

**End of the villages.** The Spaniards arrived on 27 June 1776. On **12 August 1776** the Ssalson of San Mateo attacked the Yelamu and "burned not only Sitlintac, on the shore of Mission Creek, but also two other Yelamu villages" (Palóu, in Milliken p.96). Survivors fled on tule rafts to the East Bay. Some returned in the autumn and camped at the beach. The first adult baptisms came on 24 June 1777, with Chamis of Chutchui and two boys from Sitlintac. Guimas was baptized on 2 May 1784, and the last 11 Yelamu in 1785–87. The CSV therefore ends Sitlintac at 1776.6, Chutchui at 1784.4, and the other villages at 1785 (estimated).

### 1769–1776: exploration
Portolá's scouts sighted the bay on 1–2 Nov 1769 but built nothing in San Francisco. Anza camped at Mountain Lake (Laguna del Presidio) on 27–29 Mar 1776, planted a cross at Fort Point, and named the Arroyo and Laguna de los Dolores (Eldredge i.132–135). **No structures are recorded in San Francisco between 1769 and June 1776** apart from the Yelamu villages.

### 1776–1834: Presidio and Mission
- **27 June 1776.** Moraga's party of about 75 people arrived. Milliken counts 14 soldiers, 7 settlers, their families, 2 friars, 13 Indian servants, and 286 cattle. The NPS tour says "193 colonists and soldiers". They pitched **15 tents** by the lagoon next to Chutchui, and the first mass was said under a brush arbour on 29 June. On 26 July most moved to the Presidio site. There the San Carlos sailors (from 18 Aug) built "a chapel, a storehouse, and quarters for the troops, all of wood, and thatched with rushes" (Eldredge i.151). The Presidio was formally founded on 17 Sep 1776 and the mission dedicated on 9 Oct 1776. By 31 Dec 1776 the garrison and mission had 62 men: 38 soldiers and officers, 8 settlers, 13 sailors and servants, 2 priests and a storekeeper, plus families (Eldredge ii.717).
- The **mission** stood first by the lagoon. It was a palisade chapel with quarters, a stockade and a corral. The State Historical Landmark marker puts it at Camp & Albion; Hendry & Bowman put it near 14th & Mission. In 1783 it moved to the present site at Dolores & 16th. The adobe church there was built from 1788 and dedicated in 1791 (see §4).
- **Mission Indian population, year-end** (Milliken Table F1): 1777 32; 1780 176; 1783 215; 1785 250; 1790 525; **1792 622**; 1795 872; 1800 635; 1805 1,139; **1806 886** (the measles year); 1810 1,057; 1811–13 1,205–1,224 (peak); 1817 1,060; 1820 884; 1821 1,228; 1823 208, after transfers to San Rafael and Sonoma; 1825 238; 1830 219; **1834 136**. Bancroft counts 1,252 on the registers in 1820, but that figure includes San Rafael.
- **Presidio population** (gente de razón, including the mission guards): 144 at the first census, Dec 1790 (Eldredge ii.719). In 1796–1800 the jurisdiction had 225, including 25 Catalan volunteers and 7–8 artillerymen (Bancroft i.693). Presidio padrones ranged from 200 to 360 over the years, and women and children were 70% in 1798 (Presidio tour). The jurisdiction had 330 in 1810 (Bancroft ii.126), 380 in 1817, about 430 in 1820 including 50 San Blas infantry, and 280 in 1828 (Bancroft ii.371).
- **Batería de Yerba Buena (San José)** stood on Point San José / Black Point from 1797. It was a brushwood-fascine earthwork with 8 embrasures and five 8-pounders inside a palisade. A storm levelled the palisade in 1804, and by 1806 it was useless (Bancroft i.701–702, ii.127–128).
- **Outlying households.** From the 1810s–20s, presidio families farmed **El Polín** spring in Tennessee Hollow. The Briones and Miramontes families lived there; Juana Briones c.1815–30. Russian sailors reputedly built a timber house there. Archaeologists found a stone-founded adobe and a kiln (Voss 2003). **Apolinario Miranda** built a house at the **Ojo de Agua de Figueroa** spring (Lyon St between Vallejo and Green) before 1833–38.
- **Mission pasture walls.** Mission Indians built one or two adobe or stone walls between the Potrero Nuevo and the Potrero Viejo. One ran along Precita Creek (≈ Cesar Chavez St) and one along the SW foot of Potrero Hill (BVHP p.25). Horses were corralled on Potrero Hill. The milking sheds stood on Dolores Creek at today's Mission High School (Cleary).

### 1834–1846: secularization, ranchos, Yerba Buena
- **Mission Dolores** was secularized in 1834–35. Its population was 136 in 1834, and only 8 Indian Christians were resident in 1842 (Cleary; De Mofras estimated 50). Its buildings were partly reused: part of the quadrangle became an inn and tavern (the later Mansion House), one mill became a house, a tannery was rebuilt as a house, and the rancheria was "occupied by remaining Indians" (Dean, in MDNA p.30). The rest decayed: in 1844 "the material of unoccupied buildings at the mission has been carried off" (Bancroft iv.669). Californio families built adobes and frame houses around it: De Haro, Guerrero, Noé, Valencia, Bernal and others. In 1839–40 the juzgado (court and municipal office) moved from the Presidio to the Mission. Lots were granted at Dolores from 1840.
- **Ranchos.** **Laguna de la Merced** went to J. A. Galindo on 23 Sep 1835 and was sold to Francisco de Haro on 12 May 1837. No building is located. **Rincón de las Salinas y Potrero Viejo** went to J. C. Bernal, who ran cattle there from the mid-1830s; the grant dates from 1839. Its corral stood in the gap between Mt St Joseph and Candlestick Hill. Bernal himself lived in an adobe on Duncan St near San Jose Ave and Valencia. **Las Camaritas**, 300 varas square near 14th–15th & Mission, went to Noé on 21 Jan 1840, and he built a wood house there. **San Miguel**, one league, went to Noé in Dec 1845; his adobe stood near 23rd & Douglass. **Ojo de Agua de Figueroa** was a 100-vara grant to Miranda. **Potrero Nuevo** went to the De Haro twins in 1844, with no buildings known. **Cañada de Guadalupe, la Visitación y Rodeo Viejo** went to Leese in 1841 and then Ridley. It lay mostly in San Mateo County, and the house site is unknown.
- **Presidio.** The company moved to Sonoma in 1835. From 1836 to 1840, 2–6 soldiers lived there with their families; Edwards (1837) saw "half-a-dozen families living at the ruined presidio". Mesa and 12 men garrisoned it in 1840–43. Simpson (1841) called it "a square of huts". By Feb 1844 it was in ruins, with its tiles and timber taken for the new custom house (Bancroft iii.700–706, iv.666–669).
- **Yerba Buena.** Richardson put up a tent in June 1835, and his adobe Casa Grande followed in 1837. Leese's frame house was finished on 4 Jul 1836, and Leese's beach store came in 1837–38. Spear's Kent Hall dates from 1838 and his store from 1838–39. In 1839 came Caceres's adobe, Thompson's hide house, Fuller's three houses, J. C. Davis's house and shop, Prudon's adobe, and the Spear & Hinckley mule mill. Vioget surveyed the town in 1839, covering Pacific–California × Montgomery–Dupont (Grant). He built his house and billiard saloon in 1840–41, and Hinckley built his adobe in 1840. The HBC bought Leese's store in Aug 1841. Sherreback built in 1843; Diaz, Finch and the Custom House date from 1844–45; Ridley and Leidesdorff from 1845; the City Hotel was begun in 1846. Bancroft's text lists all of these.

### 1846–1849: the American town
- On 9 Jul 1846 Montgomery raised the US flag on the custom-house pole, and Fort Montgomery battery was built at Clark's Point. On 31 Jul about 230–240 **Brooklyn** Mormons arrived and camped "among the sand-hills close to the beach". Over the winter they lodged in Richardson's Casa Grande and the old mission buildings, then built houses in 1847. On 30 Jan 1847 Yerba Buena was renamed San Francisco, and O'Farrell's survey followed in Mar–Jul 1847, swinging the grid 2.5°. The beach-lot sale was held on 20–23 Jul 1847. The first school house was built in Dec 1847. Clark built his wharf in 1847–48, and the Clay St pier was extended in Oct 1847–Jan 1848.
- **1849.** Central (Long) Wharf was begun in May 1849, and the Telegraph Hill signal station went up early in the year. The year brought the Parker House, the El Dorado tent, beached storeships such as the Niantic, and tent districts: Happy Valley had about 1,000 tents by winter 1849–50, and Sydney Town, Little Chile, Spring Valley and the Washerwoman's Lagoon laundries also grew up. The great fires of 24 Dec 1849, 4 May 1850, 14 Jun 1850 and 3–4 May 1851 destroyed almost every pre-gold building. Bancroft says that by 1852 only the 1847 Mowry adobe survived (v.677, v.686).

### 4.2 Castillo de San Joaquín (excluded from the CSV)
- Begun Aug 1793 by a gunner's mate, a master carpenter, about 30 neophytes and 30 gentiles; adobes, bricks and tiles were made on site at 1,500 adobes a day. **Completed and blessed 8 Dec 1794**, at a cost of $6,000–6,491.
- Built on the extreme point of the Cantil Blanco, a serpentine cliff about 100 ft high. **Horseshoe-shaped, about 100 × 120 ft** (Bancroft i.700); Eldredge gives "about 125 × 105 ft, parapet ten feet thick, 14 embrasures lined with brick, a barrack for the artillerymen in the middle". It was of adobe with brick-faced embrasures, plus a sentry box, a casemate and a powder house; 8 guns were mounted in 1794. Six bronze guns cast in Lima survive, at the Officers' Club, Pershing Square and Stilwell Hall.
- 1796: Córdoba reported that it rested on sand and its walls crumbled at every salute. Repaired by Jun 1797 with 6 guns in front and 3 on each side. The walls fell in a 1799 rainstorm. **1805: surrounded on three sides by a stone wall and on one by a palisade, with a new casemate 300 yards away.** By 1810 the merlons and esplanade were destroyed. **Rebuilt in 1816** with convict labour (200 beams, 600 planks, 3,600 spikes, lime mortar); 20 guns by 1820. Abandoned in 1835; one corporal in charge in 1837. Frémont spiked its guns on 1 Jul 1846. The cliff was cut down for Fort Point in 1853.

### 4.3 Mission Dolores church (excluded) and compound (in the CSV as estimates)
- **Temporary chapels.** (1) A brush enramada, 29 Jun 1776. (2) A palisade chapel with dwelling, stockade, corral and later an aqueduct at the first site, Aug–Oct 1776; official size 18 × 5 varas. (3) A second, larger palisade chapel on the same site, 1782. (4) A palisade chapel at the present site from 1783, damaged by a storm in Jan 1787 and rebuilt. The CSV includes (2) as `mis1_chapel`; **drop it if the church model already covers the first chapel.**
- **Present church.** The first stone was laid on 25 Apr 1782, or 1783 according to MDNA. Adobe-making ran from 1788 (26,000–36,000 bricks); the walls were up by 1790 and the church was dedicated in 1791 (2 Aug per Cleary, 2 Apr per MDNA). Roof tiles went on in 1794–95. Interior **114 ft × 22 ft, 21 ft to the ceiling**; walls 4 ft thick, 10 ft on the Dolores St front; foundations 4 × 4 ft of stone; three bells in façade niches; redwood rafters with Ohlone-painted chevrons. The cemetery adjoins it to the south and west ("next to the church, facing the garden", Roquefeuil). Moraga was reburied inside in 1791.
- **Compound** (CSV rows `mis_*`; layout reconstructed from the descriptions below):
  - 1792, Vancouver: the buildings formed "two sides of a square, without any apparent intention of completing the quadrangle", thatched. The Indian huts were "of willow poles, basket-work of twigs, and thatch of grass and tules, about twelve feet high, six or seven feet in diameter".
  - 1793: 19 adobe houses for neophyte families. 1794: 20 more, and a **storehouse 150 ft long with a tile roof**. 1795: **another adobe building 180 ft long**; tile roofs completed on all structures, including the church. By 1798 there were enough houses for most married neophytes.
  - Hendry & Bowman's Spanish-period list (via Market & Octavia): chapel quadrangle housing padres and servants, kitchen house, granary, **monjerío** (dormitory for unmarried women), cart shed, bathhouse, pottery, workshop, blacksmith, washroom, henhouse, 37 adobe houses for neophytes, 2 mills, 2 aqueducts. Their full study counts **47 buildings and structures**, adding barracks, a soap factory, a loom house, an adobe-brick works, a carpenter shop, storerooms, tanneries, a prison, a school and the rancheria.
  - 1806, Langsdorff: the padres' dwelling had spacious apartments; "behind that dwelling was a large court surrounded by buildings in which the neophytes were employed, chiefly in the preparation of wool and the weaving". The rancheria lay "about a hundred yards from the mission ... eight long rows of dwellings". There were tallow and soap houses, smithies, carpenter and cabinet shops, magazines and grain storehouses.
  - 1816–17, Choris, Kotzebue, Chamisso and Roquefeuil: "the church ... joins the house of the missionaries"; 20 looms; 2 mule mills; the Indian houses were "long low houses built of bricks, and forming several streets". Roquefeuil: about 100 cabins in "a rectangle divided evenly into ten blocks by four parallel streets cut across by one perpendicular". A canal crossed an empty space between the village and the workshops, then came a plaza, then "the buildings of the mission, which include the church, the lodging for the Fathers, the storehouses and the houses ... for the detachment of guards. All these buildings form a solid line parallel to the village."
  - 1827, Duhaut-Cilly: church, storehouses and living quarters form a quadrangle round an open square; beyond them, across a large court with a stream of fresh water, the Indian habitations stand "in an orderly way and divided by straight streets"; many of the structures were falling into ruin.
  - After 1834 the uses changed as described in §2. The convento was split between the **Mansion House** tavern (operating 1850) and a two-storey wooden rectory; it was razed by 1876 for the brick church on the Basilica site. 16th St was cut through the grounds in 1889.
- **Reconstruction choices made in the CSV, all low confidence.** The convento is a 40 × 10 m wing on the north side of the church, fronting Dolores St. The two tiled ranges (1794: 46 m; 1795: 55 m) close a court of about 40 m west and north of it, so the north range falls under today's 16th St. The guard houses stand at the north end of the line. The rancheria is 8 adobe ranges of 72 × 6 m, parallel to Dolores St, in the block east of Dolores between 16th and 17th. That block is the Notre Dame Plaza site at 347 Dolores, the archaeological rancheria (Ambro 2003). The workshops are in the block 15th–16th × Dolores–Guerrero, where the 1853 Coast Survey shows buildings. Long outbuildings stand at 14th–15th × Dolores–Guerrero, shown on the 1874 map.

## 6. How `early_sf.csv` was built

- **Columns.** `id,name,start_year,end_year,lat,lon,height_m,footprint_m,rotation_deg,storeys,material,count,spread_m,confidence,shape_note,source_url`. `end_year` is exclusive, as in the viewer: shown iff `start ≤ year < end`. Fractional years mark months; for example, 1776.6 is the 12 Aug 1776 burning and 1851.34 is the fire of 4 May 1851. `footprint_m` is "L×W" with L along `rotation_deg`, a compass bearing where 0 is N–S.
- **Grid bearings.** Kearny St bears 170.7° and Clay St 80.5°, so downtown rows use **171 / 81**. Dolores St bears 175° and 16th St 85°, so Mission rows use **175 / 85**. Diagonal buildings (the Casa Grande, Leese's 1836 house, Ridley's cottage) faced the pre-1847 Calle de la Fundación; their rotation of 30° or 36° is an estimate.
- **Group rows** (`count > 1`) are scatters of identical small structures within `spread_m` of the point. Exceptions: the rancheria ranges are 8 explicit rows, and the two pasture walls are single long, thin footprints whose point is the wall's centre. Stockades and corrals ("…x…" with height ≤ 2.5 m) should be drawn as fence outlines, not solid blocks. The 1797 battery and Fort Montgomery are earthworks; their material is coded `adobe` only because the column allows a fixed list.
- **Positions.** Every point comes from **present-day DataSF intersections** through `pipeline/fire_1906.intersection`. Block-internal points are bilinear interpolations between the four corner intersections (`fx` 0 = west edge to 1 = east, `fy` 0 = south to 1 = north). The generator script is not committed; the method fits in this paragraph. Points were checked visually against the street centrelines. Yerba Buena lots follow Bancroft's plan notes. Bancroft numbered lots 1–2–3 west to east along the south street of a block and 4–5–6 along the north street; Kent Hall in lot 3 (NW corner of Clay & Montgomery) and Sherreback in lot 4 (SE corner of Washington & Kearny) confirm this. His block numbers, as reconstructed here from the anchored buildings:

Bold blocks are anchored by a building whose street corner is known from Eldredge or Brown. The others, and **in particular blocks 47–51** (Harris and Evans, the American House, the Mowry adobe, Joyce), are inferred from the numbering pattern and are low confidence.

- **Confidence.** `high` means location and dates are documented and the size is known or near-certain. `medium` means a documented building whose size or exact date is estimated. `low` means the location within a block, the count or the dates are estimated, or the item is legend-grade. Every estimate is stated in `shape_note`.
- **Overlap with other data.** The 1849 tent districts, the Parker House and the storeships overlap the procedural 1849 city of `sf_predecessors.py` and the extent rings in `data/manual/sf_extent_1849.csv`. The code side should suppress procedural buildings where these documented rows stand, or start the procedural 1849 buildings later. `us49_st_francis_hotel` sits on the same lot as `yb_leese_house_1836`; their dates do not overlap.

## 7. Gaps and suggestions

1. **Maps not viewed.** Swasey's 1846–47 view, the Vioget (1839) and O'Farrell/Bartlett (1847) plans, Bancroft's v.678 plan image, and the 1853 Coast Survey sheet, which draws every Mission and downtown building. Tracing Bancroft's plan alone would fix most of the low-confidence Yerba Buena positions and settle the Bancroft block numbering above.
2. **Hendry & Bowman** (*The Spanish and Mexican Adobe and Other Buildings in the Nine San Francisco Bay Counties*, 1940–45, Bancroft Library MS) is the authoritative list of the 47 Mission Dolores structures and the Mexican-era adobes, with locations. Only secondary summaries were reachable. The Mission convento and quadrangle layout in the CSV is a reasoned reconstruction, not a survey.
3. **Unlocated or omitted.** Leese's house on the Visitación rancho (probably in San Mateo County); Galindo/De Haro's house at Lake Merced (a placeholder row); the Kirby tannery (1847), Owen's brewery (1847), Harrison's four cottages and Kittleman's Rising Sun tavern (all in Brown's list without location); the 1846–47 burial ground at Vallejo & Sansome and the flagstaff on the plaza (not buildings); the 1805 casemate 300 yards from the Castillo; the Presidio farmsteads west of the walls; the Yelamu "fourth cluster", Shiti and Ompuromo; goat-keeping on Yerba Buena Island from 1842.
4. **Village sites.** Only Tubsinte (the Crocker Mound) and Petlenuc (the Crissy Field camps) rest on archaeology; Sitlintac, Chutchui and Amuctac are placed from verbal descriptions. Ohlone site records are restricted. The tribal Association of Ramaytush Ohlone should be consulted before any public labelling.
5. **Population figures conflict** between Annals, Bancroft and the *Star* for 1846–48; §3 lists the variants.
